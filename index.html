How Studi-Phy is meant to be used

Understanding physics is a loop, not a straight line.

Three steps, repeated

  1. Understand the idea. Read the plain-language explanation and the key terms.
  2. See it. Look at the diagram and connect the labels to the words.
  3. Try it. Attempt the worked example yourself before revealing the solution.

Why it works

Physics is not a list of formulas to memorise. Each equation is a sentence about how the world behaves — and it only becomes meaningful once you can picture the situation it describes.

Every page here follows the same order: concept → picture → equation → example. Skip a step and the formula feels arbitrary; follow the order and it starts to feel obvious.

About Studi-Phy

An online physics learning website built to make physics concepts clear, accessible, and engaging for students and independent learners.

Purpose

Why this site exists

Studi-Phy explains key physics topics using simple language, visual aids, formulas, real-life examples, and practice materials. The goal is not to replace a textbook or a teacher, but to give learners a second explanation — one they can read slowly, re-read, and test themselves against without anyone watching.

Many students can follow a physics lesson in class and still feel lost when they open their notes at home. Studi-Phy is written for exactly that moment.

Message

What it is trying to say

“Physics Made Clear, One Concept at a Time.”

Physics is understandable and relevant when it is explained step by step. With the right explanations, visuals, and practice, anyone can grasp important ideas and apply them in school and in everyday life.

The site deliberately moves slowly. One concept is introduced, pictured, written as an equation, and then practised — before the next one begins.

Who Studi-Phy is for

The site is written for the people most likely to need a second explanation.

Senior High School students

Especially students in the STEM strand who are meeting mechanics, electricity, and waves for the first time.

Teachers

As a source of short, plain-language explanations, diagrams, and ready-made practice items.

Independent learners

Anyone in the school community — or outside it — who wants to learn or review physics at their own pace.

Physics Topics

Five core areas, each explained in the same order: the idea, the picture, the equations, and a worked example.

Topic 1

Mechanics & Kinematics

Kinematics is the study of how things move, without asking what causes the motion. It deals with position, displacement, speed, velocity, acceleration, and time. Almost every other topic in physics builds on these ideas.

Key ideas

  • Distance vs. displacement. Distance is how far you travelled; displacement is how far you ended up from the start, in a stated direction.
  • Speed vs. velocity. Speed is a scalar (magnitude only); velocity is a vector (magnitude and direction).
  • Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes. Slowing down is still acceleration — just with the opposite sign.
  • Free fall. Near Earth’s surface, ignoring air resistance, every object accelerates downward at about g = 9.8 m/s².

Key formulas

Average velocity

v̄ = ΔdΔt

Use for steady or average motion over a time interval.

Acceleration

a = ΔvΔt

Use when velocity changes at a constant rate.

Velocity with acceleration

v = v0 + at

Use when you know the starting velocity and the acceleration.

Displacement

d = v0t + ½at²

Use when time is known but final velocity is not.

Velocity–displacement

v² = v0² + 2ad

Use when time is unknown.

Free fall

g ≈ 9.8 m/s²

Use for objects falling with only gravity acting on them.

Worked example

Step 1 — List what is given. v0 = 0 m/s, a = 2.0 m/s², t = 5.0 s.

Step 2 — Find the final velocity. v = v0 + at = 0 + (2.0)(5.0) = 10 m/s.

Step 3 — Find the distance. d = v0t + ½at² = 0 + ½(2.0)(5.0)² = ½(2.0)(25) = 25 m.

Check. Average velocity is (0 + 10)/2 = 5.0 m/s, and 5.0 m/s × 5.0 s = 25 m. It matches.

Topic 2

Forces & Motion

Dynamics asks the next question: why does motion change? The answer is always a force — a push or a pull acting on an object. Newton’s three laws describe how forces and motion are connected.

Key ideas

  • First law (inertia). An object keeps doing what it is doing unless a net external force acts on it.
  • Second law. Net force equals mass times acceleration: Fnet = ma.
  • Third law. Forces come in pairs: if A pushes B, B pushes A back with equal strength in the opposite direction.
  • Weight is not mass. Mass is measured in kilograms; weight is a force, W = mg, measured in newtons.

Key formulas

Newton’s second law

Fnet = ma

Use whenever a net force causes acceleration.

Weight

W = mg

Use to convert mass (kg) into weight (N).

Friction

f = μN

Use for sliding contact; μ depends on the two surfaces.

Momentum

p = mv

Use for collisions and explosions.

Impulse

FΔt = Δp

Use when a force acts for a measured time.

Worked example

Step 1 — Find the net force. The push and friction oppose each other: Fnet = 20 − 8.0 = 12 N.

Step 2 — Apply F = ma. 12 = (4.0)a, so a = 12 / 4.0 = 3.0 m/s² in the direction of the push.

Check. Weight is 4.0 × 9.8 = 39 N, far larger than the push, so the box stays on the floor. The answer is reasonable.

Topic 3

Energy & Work

Energy is the capacity to do work. It cannot be created or destroyed — only transferred or transformed. That single sentence, the conservation of energy, is one of the most powerful tools in physics.

Key ideas

  • Work is done when a force moves an object along the direction of the force.
  • Kinetic energy is the energy of motion; it depends on the square of the speed.
  • Gravitational potential energy depends on height above a reference point.
  • Power is the rate of doing work — energy per unit time, measured in watts.

Key formulas

Work

W = Fd cosθ

Use when the force is at an angle to the motion.

Kinetic energy

KE = ½mv²

Use for any moving object.

Potential energy

PE = mgh

Use for objects raised above a reference level.

Power

P = Wt

Use to compare how quickly work is done.

Worked example

Step 1 — Compare energies. All the potential energy becomes kinetic energy: PEtop = KEbottom.

Step 2 — Cancel the mass. mgh = ½mv² → v² = 2gh. Mass cancels — the speed is the same for any mass.

Step 3 — Substitute. v² = 2(9.8)(5.0) = 98, so v ≈ 9.9 m/s.

Check. That is about 36 km/h — plausible for a 5 m drop.

Topic 4

Electricity & Electrostatics

Electric charge is a property of matter. Charges at rest produce electric forces (electrostatics); charges that move produce electric current.

Key ideas

  • Like charges repel, opposite charges attract.
  • Coulomb’s law gives the force between two point charges; it weakens with the square of the distance.
  • Voltage is the energy per unit charge; current is the rate of charge flow; resistance opposes that flow.
  • Series circuits share one path; parallel circuits offer several paths.

Key formulas

Ohm’s law

V = IR

Use for any ohmic conductor at constant temperature.

Electrical power

P = IV

Use to find energy per second in a component.

Coulomb’s law

F = kq1q2r²

Use for the force between two point charges.

Electric field

E = Fq

Use for force per unit charge at a point.

Worked example

Step 1 — Find the current. I = V / R = 12 / 4.0 = 3.0 A.

Step 2 — Find the power. P = IV = (3.0)(12) = 36 W.

Check. Using P = I²R = (3.0)²(4.0) = 36 W. Both methods agree.

Topic 5

Waves & Sound

A wave transfers energy from one place to another without transferring matter. Sound, light, and water ripples are all waves, described by the same handful of quantities.

Key ideas

  • Amplitude is the maximum displacement from rest — it relates to loudness or brightness.
  • Wavelength (λ) is the distance between two matching points on a wave.
  • Frequency (f) is the number of waves passing a point each second, in hertz.
  • Period (T) is the time for one complete wave; T = 1/f.

Key formulas

Wave speed

v = fλ

Use for any wave when frequency and wavelength are known.

Period

T = 1f

Use to convert frequency into time per cycle.

Snell’s law

n1sinθ1 = n2sinθ2

Use when light bends between two materials.

Worked example

Step 1 — Find the speed. v = fλ = (440)(0.78) ≈ 343 m/s.

Step 2 — Find the period. T = 1/f = 1/440 ≈ 0.0023 s (2.3 ms).

Check. 343 m/s is the accepted speed of sound in air at about 20 °C — the numbers are consistent.

Practice & Examples

Try each problem on paper first. Then open the solution and compare your steps — not just your final answer.

Worked examples

Three problems, each solved step by step with a check at the end.

Given: v0 = 0, v = 8.0 m/s, t = 4.0 s.

Acceleration: a = Δv / Δt = (8.0 − 0) / 4.0 = 2.0 m/s².

Distance: d = v0t + ½at² = 0 + ½(2.0)(4.0)² = ½(2.0)(16) = 16 m.

Check: average speed = (0 + 8.0)/2 = 4.0 m/s, and 4.0 × 4.0 = 16 m. Correct.

Given: m = 1500 kg, v0 = 20 m/s, v = 0, d = 50 m.

Find acceleration first (time is unknown): v² = v0² + 2ad → 0 = 400 + 2a(50).

So 100a = −400, giving a = −4.0 m/s² (negative because it opposes motion).

Force: F = ma = (1500)(−4.0) = −6000 N, i.e. 6000 N opposite to the direction of travel.

Check: 6000 N is about 0.4 times the car’s weight — a strong but realistic braking force.

Step 1 — Find the equivalent resistance. For two resistors in parallel: 1/R = 1/6.0 + 1/3.0 = 0.1667 + 0.3333 = 0.5000.

So R = 1/0.5000 = 2.0 Ω.

Step 2 — Use Ohm’s law. I = V / R = 12 / 2.0 = 6.0 A.

Check: branch currents are 12/6.0 = 2.0 A and 12/3.0 = 4.0 A, which add to 6.0 A. Correct — parallel branches share current.

Study Tips

Short, practical advice on how to study physics effectively — most of it is about habits, not talent.

Tip 1

Draw the situation before touching a formula

Sketch the object, the surfaces, and every force or velocity as an arrow. Most wrong answers in physics come from a wrong picture, not wrong algebra.

Tip 2

Write down what is given and what is asked

Two short lists: “known” and “find”. Then look for the one equation that connects them. This turns a word problem into a matching exercise.

Tip 3

Track your units the whole way

If the units do not come out to m/s², you probably divided when you should have multiplied. Units are a free error check on every problem.

Tip 4

Ask whether the answer is reasonable

A bicycle does not travel at 300 m/s. A 2 kg box does not weigh 2 N. A quick sanity check catches many mistakes before your teacher does.

Tip 5

Practise solving, not rereading

Reading notes feels productive but tests recognition, not recall. Close the page and try to redo a worked example from memory — that is where learning happens.

Tip 6

Study in short, spaced sessions

Twenty-five focused minutes a day beats a four-hour cram. Returning to a topic after a day is what moves it into long-term memory.

Problem-solving checklist

Run through this every time you solve a physics problem.

  1. Picture it. Sketch the situation and label everything you know.
  2. List knowns and unknowns with their units.
  3. Choose the equation that links them — and say why it applies.
  4. Rearrange before substituting so the algebra stays clean.
  5. Calculate, keeping units in every line.
  6. Check the size, sign, and units of your answer.
A reminder about honesty

These notes are study aids, not shortcuts. Use them to understand a method, then close the page and do the problem yourself — otherwise the understanding will not stick when it counts.
